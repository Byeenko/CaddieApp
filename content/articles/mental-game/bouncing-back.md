> [!key] Key points
> - Give yourself a **short window to react**, then let it go.
> - The **next shot** is the only one you can control.
> - After a bad shot, choose the **safe play**, not the hero shot.
> - Look for the **lesson**, not the blame.

## Why one bad hole becomes three
A double bogey on its own rarely ruins a round. What ruins rounds is the **chain reaction**: frustration leads to a rushed tee shot, which leads to a risky recovery, which leads to a triple. Stopping the chain is a skill you can learn.

## A simple reset
1. **React.** It's fine to feel annoyed. Give yourself until you put the club back in the bag, or about ten paces.
2. **Breathe.** One slow, deep breath, longer out than in.
3. **Learn.** One quick thought: what happened? (Rushed? Wrong club? Bad luck?)
4. **Let go.** Literally picture leaving it on the ground behind you.
5. **Re-focus** on the next target.

## After a bad shot: play smart
The moment after a poor shot is when you're most tempted to gamble, and most likely to make it worse.
- In trouble? **Get back in play first** (see [[recovery-shots]]).
- Bogey or double is still a fine score compared with a triple.
- **Use your routine** on the next shot, exactly as normal.

## Keep perspective
- Even the best players hit poor shots every round. Tour players miss around a third of greens.
- Count the **good shots** too. Most golfers remember only the bad ones.
- Your **next round** doesn't care about this one.

> [!mistake] Fixing your swing mid-round
> After a bad shot, trying a new swing thought on the next tee usually makes things worse. Stick with your normal tempo and routine. Save swing work for the range.

> [!tip] The bounce-back stat
> Track how often you make par or better on the hole after a bogey or worse. Tour players watch this "bounce-back" stat closely. Watching it improve is a great sign your mental game is getting stronger.

> [!drill] Practise recovering
> On the range, deliberately hit one bad shot (a big slice, say), then go through your reset and hit a normal shot to a target. It sounds odd, but practising the reset makes it automatic on the course.
