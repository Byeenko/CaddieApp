> [!key] Key points
> - **Turn your shoulders** around your spine. Don't lift your arms.
> - **Hinge your wrists** so the shaft and lead arm form roughly an L by the time your lead arm is parallel to the ground.
> - Let your weight move **into your trail side**, staying on the inside of your right foot.
> - At the top, a **flat lead wrist** usually means a square clubface.

## The turn

Power in the golf swing comes from rotation, not from the arms. In the backswing, your **shoulders turn around 90°** and your **hips around 45°**, though flexibility varies a lot. The difference between the two is what stores energy, like winding up a spring.

- Turn your back to the target.
- Keep your **spine angle** from address. Don't stand up or dip down.
- Let your lead knee move slightly towards the ball as your hips turn.

> [!tip] If you're not flexible
> It's fine to let your hips turn more, or to let your lead heel lift slightly off the ground. Jack Nicklaus did both. A fuller turn with some hip movement beats a restricted turn that forces your arms to take over.

## The wrist hinge

As the club passes hip height, your wrists hinge upwards. By the time your **lead arm is parallel to the ground**, the shaft should point roughly up, making an "L" with your arm. The hinge creates leverage that you release through impact.

## Weight and pressure

Your weight shifts into your **trail side** as you turn, but it should stay on the **inside of your right foot**. If it rolls to the outside of your foot, or your hips sway away from the target, you've slid rather than turned.

## At the top

| Check | What you want |
| --- | --- |
| Shoulders | Turned so your back faces the target |
| Lead arm | Fairly straight, but not rigid |
| Trail elbow | Folded, pointing roughly down towards the ground |
| Lead wrist | Flat. A cupped wrist means an open face; a bowed wrist means a closed face |
| Club length | Around parallel to the ground or a little short of it for most golfers |

> [!mistake] Over-swinging
> Letting the club drop well past parallel, usually by collapsing the lead arm or the wrists, feels powerful but costs control and timing. A shorter, fully turned backswing almost always hits it further and straighter.

> [!tour] On tour
> Backswings vary a lot on tour. Jon Rahm's is famously short, while others go past parallel. What they share is a full shoulder turn and a stable lower body. The length of the arm swing is personal.

> [!drill] Pause at the top
> Make slow swings and hold at the top for two seconds. Check the flat lead wrist and your weight on the inside of your trail foot. Then swing down and hit the ball. The pause also trains a smoother transition. See [[tempo-and-rhythm]].
